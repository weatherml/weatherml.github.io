  • Discovering Equations for the Thermodynamics of South Asian Monsoon Precipitation¶

    Savannah L. Ferretti, Jane W. Baldwin, Michael S. Pritchard, Tom Beucler · Nov 2026

    South Asian monsoon precipitation depends on the thermodynamic structure of the lower troposphere, but how its ingredients combine to shape precipitation remains unclear, partly because this...South Asian monsoon precipitation depends on the thermodynamic structure of the lower troposphere, but how its ingredients combine to shape precipitation remains unclear, partly because this dependence is nonlocal in the vertical. We use symbolic distillation, which combines neural networks and symbolic regression, to discover interpretable precipitation equations from 21 years of June-August reanalysis data. Neural networks first learn the vertical thermodynamic structure most relevant to precipitation, condensing each variable into a single interpretable feature not tied to a particular vertical grid. Then, symbolic regression derives closed-form equations from these features and the surface state. Our best equation explains 1.5 times as much precipitation variance as a widely used buoyancy-based diagnostic and recovers about 85% of neural network skill while using about a thousand times fewer parameters. Unlike the buoyancy diagnostic, which adds moisture and instability, the equations treat them as competing controls, with the heaviest precipitation occurring when the lower free troposphere is humid. Over ocean, sensible heating is associated with additional precipitation mainly when near-surface air is warm and moist, improving predicted precipitation and its diurnal cycle. Although the learned features were optimized only for precipitation prediction, the equations largely respond to each feature as expected from convective physics, consistent with the reanalysis. These responses reduce the dependence of monsoon precipitation on the entire vertical column to a few simple, testable constraints that could guide data-driven precipitation models to make them more physically consistent. more

  • PARDIG: Global Precipitation Estimates Learned from Sparse Satellite Observations¶

    Guy Shalev, Stephan Rasp, Boris Babenko, Andrew El-Kadi, Mohammed Alewi Hassen, Samier Merchant et al. · Nov 2026

    Estimating precipitation at the global scale is vital for Earth system science and disaster response, yet observational coverage and measurement precision remain constrained. Existing solutions...Estimating precipitation at the global scale is vital for Earth system science and disaster response, yet observational coverage and measurement precision remain constrained. Existing solutions provide options that trade off desirable traits such as skill, latency, geographical coverage, temporal coverage, and spatial and temporal resolution. We present PARDIG, an artificial intelligence framework based on a spherical graph-mesh transformer architecture that produces near-real-time global precipitation estimates. PARDIG is trained on dense multi-modal inputs, including atmospheric state analyses and multi-spectral geostationary satellite imagery, to predict sparse, high-precision precipitation measurements from spaceborne radar. At inference, the trained model is applied densely across the globe at \(0.1^\circ\) spatial and 15-minute temporal resolution, producing complete near-real-time precipitation fields from 2016 to the present, alongside a historical variant extending the record back to 1999. Evaluation against three independent radar networks (over the US, Europe, and Japan) and against over 36,000 surface stations worldwide shows that PARDIG consistently outperforms IMERG V07 (Final and Early), MSWEP V3, ERA5, and HRES across precipitation thresholds and regions, with the largest improvements at extreme intensities. Case studies of extreme precipitation events demonstrate that PARDIG successfully captures their spatial structure and timing. The improvements also extend to forecasting: WeatherNext 3, a global weather model, produces more skillful precipitation forecasts when trained to predict PARDIG. Both the historical and near-real-time PARDIG datasets are openly available under a CC BY 4.0 license. more

  • MarsNet: Learning the Martian Atmosphere from Observations Alone¶

    Sebastian Mengwall, J. Michael Battalio · Nov 2026

    We present MarsNet, a machine-learned model of the Martian atmosphere trained only on observations. Existing reanalyses assimilate spacecraft retrievals into general circulation models (GCMs), so...We present MarsNet, a machine-learned model of the Martian atmosphere trained only on observations. Existing reanalyses assimilate spacecraft retrievals into general circulation models (GCMs), so where observations are sparse their fields largely reflect the model. We therefore use extant reanalyses only for evaluation. MarsNet alternates a two-hour forecast with an update that assimilates the observations of each window, and both steps are learned jointly. Because the Martian record is too sparse for an unrestricted network, learning is confined within a fixed physical structure: the state is in physical units, the forecast uses physical tendencies of fixed form and learned strength, and the update is a bounded Kalman update. MarsNet is trained on Mars Years (MY) 24--31 and evaluated on independent observations. On three seasons of MGS radio-occultation temperatures assimilated by no dataset, MarsNet matches or improves on the three reanalyses in the lowest three scale heights, with a near-surface root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 4.2 K against 4.4--5.4 K, but is less accurate aloft. The model's surface pressures are the most accurate of the four products (RMSE 10 Pa against Curiosity in MY32, compared with 20--46 Pa). On withheld Mars Climate Sounder profiles of MY32, its temperatures beat an observed climatology and persistence, including in ten-sol forecasts without new observations, and its stated uncertainty is close to calibrated. Much of the temperature skill is present before training and appears to come from the structure and tables fitted to the observations, while training mainly improves the surface pressures, at some cost to dust. The model cycles stably through nine Mars years, and the influence of each observation can be traced. Its speed makes experiments on the observing record practical, and it offers an independent reference for GCMs and reanalyses. more

  • Analysis increments for model bias diagnosis and correction: Theoretical foundations¶

    Eviatar Bach · Nov 2026

    A common method for diagnosing and correcting model bias in weather and climate prediction is based on analysis increments: the difference between the analysis and the forecast at each data...A common method for diagnosing and correcting model bias in weather and climate prediction is based on analysis increments: the difference between the analysis and the forecast at each data assimilation cycle. Their time average is used to estimate and correct the bias, and machine learning is increasingly used to map the forecast state to the increments, giving a state-dependent correction. Despite its widespread use, the method lacks theoretical foundations. We first analyse a Kalman filter whose forecast model has a constant bias. We show what the averaged increment measures, and prove that iterating the correction removes all of the bias seen through the observations; for linear dynamics, no method can identify the rest.     Stefan Siegert · Nov 2026

    AI weather prediction models are released with 32-bit (fp32) weights and run in fp32 by default, although current GPUs compute much faster in 16-bit formats. We run five publicly available models --...AI weather prediction models are released with 32-bit (fp32) weights and run in fp32 by default, although current GPUs compute much faster in 16-bit formats. We run five publicly available models -- Aurora, Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet, SFNO and FastNet -- on a single 16 GB GPU from 52 initial dates in 2024, in fp32 and with their networks in half precision (fp16), and verify the forecasts against ERA5. The physical state must stay in fp32: converting the whole model rounds it in physical units, which in fp16 overflows and in bf16 discards meaningful information. With the state, its normalisation and denormalisation in fp32, running the network in fp16 changes the global RMSE by at most 1.4%, and by about 0.5% or less for four of the five models, equivalent to less than one hour of lead time up to day 8 and at most two hours at day 10. The differences show no systematic pattern by region, scale or for tropical and extratropical storms, apart from a small tropical penalty in FourCastNet, and fp16 leaves the CRPS, spread and calibration of Aurora's ensemble almost unchanged. No value overflowed fp16's numeric range (+/-65,504); the largest stay 38 to 544 times below it. A 10-day forecast in fp16 is 1.3-3.5 times faster than in fp32 and uses 1.4-3.7 times less energy. Sensitivity experiments show that rules of thumb from other applications of reduced precision carry over only partly: the first and last layers dominate for some models but not for others, and no type of layer is sensitive on its own. For running AI weather models on a single GPU, fp16 with the state in fp32 is practically lossless at a fraction of the cost. bf16, often preferred for its wider range, is as fast but less accurate than fp16 for every model, and simple 8-bit quantisation costs far more skill. more

    Artificial intelligence (AI) based ocean state forecasts now approach or surpass those produced by operational ocean forecasting systems as measured by conventional pointwise metrics. However, these metrics may favor spatially smoothed forecasts and cannot determine whether forecast variables remain physically coherent. Forecast evaluation should therefore move beyond overall pointwise accuracy toward assessing forecast trustworthiness. We propose a multidimensional framework comprising overall pointwise accuracy, physical consistency, and distributional consistency. Overall pointwise accuracy summarizes forecast and reference agreement over all valid samples; physical consistency evaluates dynamical balances using model outputs alone; and distributional consistency evaluates distribution tails and multiscale spatial structures. Three AI models were deployed as representative testbeds in a unified operational environment. This comprehensive assessment shows that lower overall pointwise errors cannot preclude substantial dynamical imbalances and suppressed fine-scale variance. Conversely, higher pointwise errors may arise from spatial displacement of coherent structures rather than a poor representation of their amplitude. The results demonstrate that the evaluation of AI ocean forecasting models should be based on the multi-dimensional assessment of forecast trustworthiness, rather than solely relying on a single metric.
