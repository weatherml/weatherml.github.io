  • PARDIG: Global Precipitation Estimates Learned from Sparse Satellite Observations¶

    Guy Shalev, Stephan Rasp, Boris Babenko, Andrew El-Kadi, Mohammed Alewi Hassen, Samier Merchant et al. · Nov 2026

    Estimating precipitation at the global scale is vital for Earth system science and disaster response, yet observational coverage and measurement precision remain constrained. Existing solutions...Estimating precipitation at the global scale is vital for Earth system science and disaster response, yet observational coverage and measurement precision remain constrained. Existing solutions provide options that trade off desirable traits such as skill, latency, geographical coverage, temporal coverage, and spatial and temporal resolution. We present PARDIG, an artificial intelligence framework based on a spherical graph-mesh transformer architecture that produces near-real-time global precipitation estimates. PARDIG is trained on dense multi-modal inputs, including atmospheric state analyses and multi-spectral geostationary satellite imagery, to predict sparse, high-precision precipitation measurements from spaceborne radar. At inference, the trained model is applied densely across the globe at \(0.1^\circ\) spatial and 15-minute temporal resolution, producing complete near-real-time precipitation fields from 2016 to the present, alongside a historical variant extending the record back to 1999. Evaluation against three independent radar networks (over the US, Europe, and Japan) and against over 36,000 surface stations worldwide shows that PARDIG consistently outperforms IMERG V07 (Final and Early), MSWEP V3, ERA5, and HRES across precipitation thresholds and regions, with the largest improvements at extreme intensities. Case studies of extreme precipitation events demonstrate that PARDIG successfully captures their spatial structure and timing. The improvements also extend to forecasting: WeatherNext 3, a global weather model, produces more skillful precipitation forecasts when trained to predict PARDIG. Both the historical and near-real-time PARDIG datasets are openly available under a CC BY 4.0 license. more

  • RainFlow: Flow Matching for Infrared-Only Quantitative Precipitation Estimation¶

    Matthieu Meignin, Cécile Mallet, Nicolas Viltard · Nov 2026

    Geostationary infrared imagers observe precipitating systems every 10 to 15 minutes over large areas, but they sense cloud tops rather than the rain beneath. Their brightness temperatures constrain...Geostationary infrared imagers observe precipitating systems every 10 to 15 minutes over large areas, but they sense cloud tops rather than the rain beneath. Their brightness temperatures constrain surface rain only weakly, so retrievals trained by regression return a smooth estimate that misses heavy rain and carries no measure of uncertainty. We treat infrared-only retrieval as conditional generation and introduce RainFlow, a flow-matching model that generates ensembles of rain fields from MSG/SEVIRI infrared observations, in a direct and a residual variant. Evaluated against radar over metropolitan France for a full year, the generated members are realistic rain fields, with the observed dry areas, heavy rain and spatial structure, and the ensemble mean is as accurate as a deterministic baseline. Used as probabilities, the ensembles locate heavy rain at scales where the baseline has no skill, and their spread captures most of the retrieval error, with only slight underdispersion. Trained with a time-sampling density that favours the noise end of the generative trajectory, the two variants reach similar skill. The direct variant depends strongly on this choice of density, whereas the residual variant is nearly insensitive to it. Evaluating the generated rain as a function of brightness temperature shows that the ensembles reproduce how rain depends on it over most of the range. It also isolates the remaining gap, an underestimate of both the amount and the variability of rain under the coldest cloud tops, which scores computed over all pixels do not reveal. more

  • A Deep-Learning Framework for the Generalized Emulation of Atmospheric Radiative Transfer at Microwave Frequencies¶

    Spencer R. Jones, Simon Pfreundschuh, Christian D. Kummerow · Nov 2026

    This paper presents an application of machine learning to the problem of the computational expense of atmospheric radiative transfer calculations from physics-based models. While in recent studies...This paper presents an application of machine learning to the problem of the computational expense of atmospheric radiative transfer calculations from physics-based models. While in recent studies individual emulators have been created that emulate certain radiative transfer schemes for different sensors, we show that a general framework can be applied to emulate the radiative transfer method itself. A fully connected deep neural network has been trained to predict top-of-atmosphere microwave radiances in all-sky conditions over various land, ocean and sea ice surfaces at all frequencies from 1 to 300 GHz, and for all incidence angles from 0 to 65 degrees. The reference models against which the emulator has been trained include an Eddington two-stream radiative transfer model for one-dimensional and pseudo-three-dimensional microwave brightness temperature simulations. This neural network architecture has also been separately trained to reproduce the explicit calculations of the Community Radiative Transfer Model (CRTM) for a single microwave imager. Compared to the explicit schemes used to produce the training data, the GPU-enabled emulator is able to produce brightness temperature simulations more than three orders of magnitude faster and with a root-mean-squared-error less than 0.0348 K, 0.0531 K, and 0.0246 K against the independent test data for the two Eddington models and CRTM, respectively. Additionally, preliminary training using a full 3D Monte Carlo scheme for high-resolution simulations shows promising results for the application of this method to "exact" radiative transfer methods, and these results are discussed in Section IV. more

Existing deep learning approaches rely primarily on gridded reanalysis or forecast data, limiting their applicability to satellite observations available for time-critical applications. We introduce SATFRONT, a machine learning framework for detecting cold, warm, and occluded fronts over Europe from geostationary satellite observations, with reanalysis data as an optional additional input.     Accurate precipitation estimation at fine spatial scales is critical for hydrology, agriculture, and climate studies. Infrared brightness temperatures from geostationary satellites offer excellent...Accurate precipitation estimation at fine spatial scales is critical for hydrology, agriculture, and climate studies. Infrared brightness temperatures from geostationary satellites offer excellent temporal coverage over continental-scale domains. However, because these measurements primarily characterize cloud-top properties rather than precipitation processes near the surface, their correlation with rainfall intensity remains limited, making quantitative precipitation estimation challenging. In this study, we conduct a systematic inter-comparison of state-of-the-art deep learning models for high-resolution precipitation retrieval from Meteosat Second Generation infrared brightness temperatures over metropolitan France. These models include deterministic U-Nets, transformer-based architectures, conditional GANs, and diffusion models. In contrast, generative models better capture the full precipitation distribution, including rare and heavy rainfall events, producing more realistic spatial structures at the cost of reduced pixel-wise fidelity. These results highlight a trade-off between pixel-wise accuracy and precipitation variability, showing that generative approaches are advantageous for extreme-event detection and probabilistic applications. This work establishes a reproducible framework for evaluating infrared- based precipitation retrieval methods and provides guidance for designing models that balance precision, variability, and extreme-event representation. more
